Liquidity — The Third Dimension

The 2×2 matrix uses growth and inflation, but the S&P 500 Macro Regime Playbook is explicit that the full regime read has three inputs: growth, inflation, and whether liquidity (monetary policy + financial conditions) is loose or tight. Liquidity is the overlay that can override the quadrant.

Ample / Loosening Liquidity

Equities prosper most in Goldilocks when liquidity is ample. Loose conditions can lift risk assets even on mediocre growth — multiples expand, credit spreads tighten, high-beta and long-duration equity lead.

Tight / Tightening Liquidity

Tightening compresses valuation multiples even when nominal earnings rise — the S&P can languish in high-inflation regimes for exactly this reason. Stagflation = weak growth + tight liquidity = the worst case.

Why it matters: a Fed pivot from tightening to easing is itself a regime change that can ignite major reversals — often before the growth/inflation data confirm the new quadrant. Read liquidity alongside the two axes, not after them.

Global Liquidity & Capital Flows

Central bank balance sheets, cross-border flows, and the dollar cycle drive all risk assets

The Twin Pillars — Duration Risk & Credit Risk

Money is a web of asset-liability relationships — every financial asset is someone else's liability. All macro risk reduces to two questions about those relationships: will I get my money back in real terms? (duration risk) and will I get my money back at all? (credit risk). Inflation amplifies the first; growth amplifies the second. Central bank policy moderates both.

Duration Risk — Inflation is the Amplifier

What it is: Uncertainty in the real purchasing power of future cash flows. A 30-yr bond promises nominal dollars; you don't know what those dollars will buy.

Drivers: Inflation level and volatility, interest rate volatility, term premium.

Where it lives: Long-duration bonds, growth/tech stocks (cash flows years out), real estate (rate-sensitive financing), TIPS basis.

Signal: Steepening curve, rising inflation breakevens, MOVE index spike, term premium expansion.

Credit Risk — Growth is the Amplifier

What it is: Uncertainty in nominal repayment — will the issuer actually pay you back?

Drivers: Borrower cash flow stability, leverage, collateral, growth environment. Nominal GDP growth is the master variable.

Where it lives: HY/junk bonds, EM debt, leveraged loans, equity (residual claim), bank loans, CRE.

Signal: HY OAS widening, IG spreads creeping up, CDX jumps, rating downgrades, weak primary market issuance.

Why this frame matters

Every regime in the dashboard maps to a combination of these two: Goldilocks = low duration risk + low credit risk (both stocks and bonds rally). Overheat = rising duration risk + falling credit risk (bonds suffer, stocks mixed). Stagflation = high duration risk + high credit risk (both fall — worst regime). Recession = falling duration risk + rising credit risk (quality bonds rally, equities fall). Diagnosing which pillar is moving tells you which assets to rotate into.

The USD Recycling Loop — Why the US Can Run Permanent Deficits

The US current account deficit is not a bug — it's the mechanism that supplies the world with USD and suppresses US long-term rates. Understanding this loop is core to the Capital Flows framework.

① US Imports
US consumers buy goods from the world (China, Germany, Japan, oil exporters). USD flows out into foreign hands — the current account deficit.
② World Earns USD
Trading partners accumulate large USD reserves. They need safe, liquid USD assets to park those reserves — domestic options are limited.
③ Recycled into UST
Foreign central banks and sovereign wealth funds buy US Treasuries. China, Japan, and petrostates are the largest holders — structural, price-insensitive buyers.
④ Long Rates Suppressed
Structural foreign demand keeps US 10yr yields lower than fiscal deficits alone would imply. This is why the US has been able to run large deficits without the bond vigilantes winning.

When the Loop Breaks

If foreign buyers reduce UST purchases (China diversifying, petrostates defending currency), structural rate suppression disappears → term premium rises → long yields climb without Fed action. Watch: TIC data monthly, foreign central bank reserve composition.

Trading Implication

Strong global trade → more USD recycled → UST demand supported → long yields capped. Trade war / de-globalization → fewer USD recycled → term premium rises → bear steepener risk. Trade policy is bond market policy.

QE / QT — How the Fed Moves Global Liquidity

The Fed's balance sheet is the master valve for global dollar liquidity. QE/QT expands or contracts it directly — affecting risk appetite and valuations across all markets, not just US bonds.

QE (Easing) — liquidity OUT

Fed buys USTs + agency MBS → adds demand and removes duration → long yields fall, term premium compresses. Injects reserves → "exports" dollars; capital chases yield abroad → surges into EM, weaker USD, risk-on. 2020: aggressive buying stabilized a collapsing bond market.

QT (Tightening) — liquidity IN

Balance sheet runs off → private investors must absorb Treasury supply → upward bias on yields, term premium expands. Dollar funding tightens globally → stress for USD borrowers. 2013 "taper tantrum": merely hinting at slowing purchases spiked the 10Y 100+ bp.

Net-liquidity shorthand (see metric panel below): Fed BS − RRP − TGA. Watch the direction of the balance sheet, not just the policy rate — QE/QT can ease or tighten conditions independent of where fed funds sits.

The Credit & Liquidity Cycle — Four Phases with Reflexivity

Distinct from the macro regime quadrants — this cycle tracks how money moves between safe and risky assets over time. Each phase is self-reinforcing through reflexivity: investor beliefs alter credit conditions → conditions alter fundamentals → fundamentals reinforce the original belief. Cycles persist longer than fundamentals justify, then reverse violently.

① Expansion — Risk-On, Spreads Compress

Growth solid, easy credit, low rates. Investors rotate OUT along the risk curve — bills → IG → HY → equities → EM → alternatives. Tight spreads spur more issuance → more risk-seeking capital → spreads even tighter. Reflexive feedback loop: success breeds confidence breeds more risk-taking. Quietly accumulating leverage in the system.

② Euphoria / Bubble — Risk Premia Vanish

Late-cycle. Risk curve flattens or inverts — even speculative assets yield little extra over safe ones. Covenant-lite debt to weak borrowers proliferates. "Goldilocks" everywhere talk. Valuations stretched, leverage hidden in plain sight. Volatility low (looks great until it breaks). The cycle sows its own reversal.

③ Contraction — Flight to Quality, Deleveraging

Trigger: rate hikes, growth stall, or external shock. Sentiment flips. Investors rotate IN — short maturities, upgrade quality, dump HY/EM. Banks tighten lending (price AND quantity). Simultaneous deleveraging → liquidity crunch → fire-sale prices. Adverse reflexivity: fear of defaults causes the defaults to happen. Correlations go to 1; everything falls except very safest cash and short Treasuries.

④ Stabilization & Recovery — Policy Stops the Bleeding

Central bank cuts aggressively, provides liquidity (QE, swap lines, facility backstops), maybe buys credit directly (Fed bought corporate ETFs in 2020). Spreads peak and start tightening. Best risk/reward opportunities of the cycle if you have cash. Liquidity trap risk: if rates already at zero and confidence broken, additional easing may not restart lending immediately (Japan post-1990, Eurozone late 2010s).

Practical use: Locate the current phase first, then position accordingly. Late-expansion = reduce leveraged exposure, upgrade quality. Mid-contraction = build cash, wait for capitulation. Stabilization = best entry for the next expansion. Most investor pain comes from being late to recognize phase transitions — which is why monitoring credit spreads, lending standards (SLOOS), and risk-curve flows matters more than waiting for the official regime to change.

When Central Banks Lose Control — Systemic Tail Scenarios

Rare but severe — when investors stop believing central banks can contain yields, currencies, or inflation. The implicit "Fed put" evaporates, and all assets must reprice to new risk premia. Recognize the signs early; the prepared survive and eventually thrive in the recovery.

Loss-of-Control Signals

→ Long yields spike rapidly despite CB intervention attempts (UK gilts Sept 2022: +150bp in days, BoE forced to intervene to halt LDI cascade)
→ Currency goes into free-fall regardless of rate hikes (Turkey, Argentina under political pressure not to hike)
→ Inflation expectations un-anchor (5y5y forward breakeven breaks >3%)
→ MOVE index sustained above 150 (bond vol regime change)
→ Auctions undersubscribed (bid-to-cover <2.0 on benchmark UST)
→ Cross-currency basis blows out (global USD funding seizes — March 2020)

Survival & Trade Playbook

Flight to real assets: Gold, commodities, energy. Performed in 1970s (gold $35 → $800) and 2022 commodities rally.
Deep OTM equity puts: SPX puts 20-30% OTM as crash insurance. Cheap in calm times, explode in vol spikes.
Long volatility: VIX call spreads, MOVE index exposure, long-dated straddles on index ETFs.
Pay rates / steepeners: Short long-dated UST, receive short. Profits if yields blow out.
CDS on weak credits: Insurance on HY indices, peripheral sovereigns. Massive payoffs if defaults wave (2008 subprime CDS).
Foreign FX / hard currency: CHF, JPY in DM crisis; USD or gold in EM crisis.
Cash is king in deflationary panic: When correlations go to 1, only cash and short Treasuries hold. Don't be a hero with leverage.

Historical case studies: 1970s US — Fed under Burns/Miller behind the curve, 10Y yields hit double digits, stocks dead in real terms for a decade, gold ×20; Volcker's 20% rates ended it at cost of severe recession. 2008 GFC — credit spreads + LIBOR-OIS waved red flags for ~12 months before equity capitulated. 2022 UK LDI — pension fund leverage cascade forced BoE emergency gilt purchases. 2020 COVID — cross-currency basis blowout = global USD shortage, Fed swap lines saved it. Rule: in true systemic events, traditional 60/40 fails; rely on non-correlated hedges set up in advance (hedges become unaffordable after the storm starts).

STIR Markets & Reading the Fed Path

Short-Term Interest Rate (STIR) futures are how the market prices the Fed path before it happens. The FedWatch tool above is built on this data. The card below explains what the futures actually represent and how to read the probabilities.

The Two Key CME Contracts

ContractUnderlyingListing
SR3 (3-Month SOFR) Average compounded SOFR over the contract's reference quarter Quarterly (Mar/Jun/Sep/Dec)
ZQ (30-Day Fed Funds) Arithmetic average of daily EFFR over the contract's calendar month Monthly

Implied rate = 100 − settlement price. Both contracts quote in IMM "100-minus-rate" convention. SR3 at 95.42 implies 4.58% SOFR for that quarter; ZQ at 96.30 implies 3.70% average EFFR for that month.

How FedWatch Builds Probabilities

Step 1 — Decompose the ZQ contract. A ZQ contract settling in a month with an FOMC meeting blends two regimes: the rate before the meeting and the rate after. Day-weighting solves for the implied post-meeting rate.
Step 2 — Compare post-meeting rate to current EFFR. Difference / 0.25 = expected number of 25bp moves.
Step 3 — Interpolate between 25bp grid points. If the implied move is 0.6 of a cut, FedWatch shows P(hold) = 40%, P(cut 25bp) = 60%.
Step 4 — Chain forward. Each meeting's post-rate becomes the prior-rate for the next, building a full path of implied probabilities across all upcoming FOMC dates.

What the SOFR strip tells you that ZQ doesn't

SOFR is a secured overnight rate (repo-based); EFFR is unsecured bank-to-bank. Most of the time they track within a few bp. When SOFR trades persistently above EFFR in futures pricing, the market is pricing funding stress / collateral scarcity into the forward path. When SOFR is well below EFFR in long-dated SR3, the market is expecting balance-sheet pressure to ease as the Fed unwinds policy.

Reading FedWatch in practice

The terminal rate on the strip = the first inflection (highest if hikes priced, lowest if cuts priced). The cumulative cuts/hikes count from today's EFFR is how aggressively the market expects the cycle to evolve. Watch for shifts day-to-day — a sudden 20bp move in the 12-month-forward implied rate after a CPI print is the market repricing the entire path, more meaningful than the headline FOMC decision itself.

Reading the SOFR−EFFR Basis on the Live Strip

The SOFR−EFFR field on the live data strip is the spread between two overnight rates measured the same day. It looks tiny — usually a handful of basis points — but it's one of the most information-dense fields on the strip. Here's how to read it.

What's in the Spread

SOFR = secured overnight repo rate against UST collateral. Reflects the cost of cash when backed by safe paper. Most lenders are non-banks (MMFs, FHLBs, foreign CBs).
EFFR = effective fed funds rate, unsecured bank-to-bank overnight lending. Administered by the Fed: sits within the target range, ~12bp below IORB (the bank-facing ceiling).
The basis isolates funding/collateral stress from policy. EFFR is mostly static between FOMC meetings; SOFR moves daily with repo conditions. So basis changes = SOFR changes = real-time funding signal.

Reading the Reading

BasisStateInterpretation
−10 to +2 bpNormalRoutine cash/collateral conditions. SOFR usually trades slightly below EFFR.
+2 to +7 bpTighteningMMF cash competing for repo; dealer balance sheet getting fuller. Watch for quarter-end.
+7 to +15 bpWarningReal funding stress building. Likely quarter-end / G-SIB scoring effect, but verify.
> +15 bpCrisisGenuine cash scarcity. Sept 2019 spiked >200bp — Fed restarted balance-sheet ops within weeks.

Why It Widens Predictably

Quarter-end seasonality. G-SIB scoring uses period-end balance-sheet snapshots. Big banks shrink balance sheets at quarter-ends (especially Dec 31) to lower their G-SIB tier next year. Less dealer capacity to absorb MMF cash → cash competes for fewer repo seats → SOFR rises → basis widens. This is benign; usually unwinds within days.

Tax dates. Corporate tax payments (Apr 15, Jun 15, Sep 15, Jan 15) drain bank reserves into the Treasury General Account. Reserves scarce → repo tighter. Sept 2019 was tax date + UST settlement combined.

Why It Widens Diagnostically

Off-cycle widening (mid-month, far from quarter-end) is the real signal. It means dealer balance sheets are constrained for non-calendar reasons — typically because reserves in the system are getting low relative to demand. The Fed's Standing Repo Facility (SRF, launched 2021) caps SOFR at the upper bound when private arbitrage fails, but persistent widening tells you the system's running close to that ceiling.

Trade implication: sustained off-cycle widening >7bp historically precedes Fed easing of QT pace or balance-sheet operations within months.

Why this isn't priced into the 3M T-bill on the strip: The 3M is a forward-looking term rate covering the next ~90 days, so it averages out daily funding spikes. The SOFR−EFFR basis is today's overnight signal. They measure different things — the basis catches plumbing stress before it can show up in the term curve.

Term Premium & Yield Curve Factors

Long-term yield = average expected short rates over the period + term premium. Term premium is the extra compensation investors demand for holding duration rather than rolling short-term instruments. It embeds inflation uncertainty, supply pressure, and risk aversion — and is not directly observable (estimated via models like ACM).

Term Premium by Regime

Crisis / Flight to Quality → TP goes negative. Bonds are so in demand as hedges that investors accept yields below expected short rate path.
QE Era / Low Volatility → TP compressed. Fed is buying duration directly; investors confident in anchored inflation.
Stagflation / Fiscal Expansion → TP explodes higher. Investors demand extra yield for inflation risk + heavy Treasury supply. Long yields rise even beyond Fed hike expectations.
Goldilocks → TP low and stable. Investors confident; carry trade works; bond returns driven by coupon, not moves.

3 Principal Yield Curve Factors

Level (Factor 1) — Parallel shift of all yields. Explains ~80% of moves. Driven by broad inflation/growth expectations and Fed credibility.
Slope (Factor 2) — Short vs. long divergence. Driven by policy expectations. Steepening = easing expected or inflation fears. Flattening = hiking or growth concerns.
Curvature (Factor 3) — Belly vs. wings. The intermediate sector moves differently from short and long ends. Often driven by term premium or technicals. Watch 2s5s10s butterfly.

P&L attribution: "Did yields rise overall (level), did the curve flatten (slope), or did the belly cheapen (curvature)?"

Trading implication: If term premiums are too low (QE-compressed) → position for normalization (short long bonds — yields rise even if Fed expectations unchanged). If term premiums are too high (fear spike) → go long bonds expecting TP to mean-revert as fear fades. Term premium makes long bonds move even without Fed action.

Carry & Roll-Down — The "Getting Paid to Wait" Framework

Carry and roll-down explain your expected return assuming the yield curve stays unchanged. They determine whether a position bleeds P&L slowly or earns income while you wait for your thesis to play out.

Carry

Coupon income minus funding cost (repo). In a normal steep curve, long bonds have positive carry — you earn more yield than the short-term funding costs. In an inverted curve, long bonds can be negative carry — you pay more to fund than you earn.

Example: 10yr yields 4%, repo rate 5.3% → negative carry of 1.3%. The curve must steepen / yields fall for you to net profit.

Roll-Down

As time passes, a bond "rolls down" the yield curve — a 10yr bond becomes a 9yr bond. If the curve is upward sloping, shorter maturities yield less, so the bond's yield falls → price rises → P&L gain. Roll-down is the price appreciation from simply "aging" down the curve.

In a steep curve: both carry AND roll-down are positive → powerful tailwind. In an inverted curve: both can be negative → position must move quickly or bleeds.

Key rule: High positive carry gives you cushion — the position earns income while waiting. Negative carry means you need the trade to pay off quickly. A steep curve is a systematic "get paid to own duration" signal; an inverted curve is the opposite — a tax on being long.

Stagflation is a scenario branch, not a sequential phase. The cycle follows emergence → acceleration → peak → moderation → disinflation.

Full risk-on + carry + long duration

Demand-Pull vs Cost-Push — Know the Source

Identifying where inflation comes from is what lets you predict the policy response and the asset impact. The two sources call for opposite playbooks.

Demand-Pull — "too much money chasing too few goods"

Aggregate demand outstrips supply; accompanies a booming economy. Can be cooled by tighter policy — rate hikes work directly. Bullish cyclicals initially, then bearish as hikes bite.

Cost-Push — rising input costs (energy, wages, materials)

Producers pass higher costs through; can occur even in a weak economy. Source of the dreaded stagflation. Rate hikes don't fix supply — the CB is cornered: fight inflation OR support growth, not both.

This is the mechanism behind the Stagflation quadrant: an oil/supply shock is cost-push, so the Fed has no clean tool. Watch inflation expectations (breakevens) — once they un-anchor, the central bank is forced into the most aggressive (Volcker-style) response regardless of source.

Critical: Stock-Bond Correlation Regime Shift

High Inflation — Correlation turns POSITIVE

Bonds no longer hedge equity risk. Both stocks AND bonds fall together in Phases II-III. Traditional 60/40 portfolios get hit from both sides. Diversifiers needed: commodities, managed futures, real assets, long vol.

Low Inflation — Correlation turns NEGATIVE

Classic stock-bond diversification works. Bonds hedge equity drawdowns. 60/40 and risk-parity portfolios perform. Bonds rally when equities sell off (flight to safety). Phases IV-V and Goldilocks regime.

Implication: The moment you enter Phase II (CPI >5%, Fed hiking), your bond allocation stops protecting you. Rotate to commodities, managed futures, or long vol as the equity hedge instead.

Volatility Structure & Dealer Gamma — Reading Market Positioning

Volatility metrics are an X-ray of market positioning. They reveal whether investors are hedged or complacent, and whether the market is structurally stable or one shock away from a cascade.

Implied vs. Realized Volatility (IV vs RV)

IV much > RV → Market is paying up for protection. Investors are over-hedged / fearful. Contrarian signal: if the feared event passes without incident, vol collapses → relief rally. Good time to sell premium.
RV spikes above IV → The market moved more than options priced. Implies a real shock. IV will catch up fast as everyone scrambles for protection. VIX explodes.
VIX Term Structure Inverted (near-term vol > far-term) → Crisis / near-term panic. Short-term options expensive. After event resolves, near-term vol collapses fast. Reverts to normal contango quickly.
High Skew (puts much more expensive than calls) → Crash insurance in demand. Market is "hedged" — a downturn may be buffered. But if vol is persistently high with no catalyst, it may be overdone. Low skew → complacency → buy cheap puts.

Dealer Gamma Dynamics

Dealers Long Gamma → They sell as market rises, buy as it falls. Creates mean-reversion. Market feels "pinned" or range-bound. Realized vol suppressed. Common when investors sell covered calls / puts (yield-seeking). Happens in calm Goldilocks.
Dealers Short Gamma → They buy as market rises, sell as it falls. Amplifies moves. "Air pocket" drops possible — market falls through put strikes, dealer selling adds fuel. Common when investors panic-buy puts or aggressively buy calls. Happens in fear/speculation regimes.
Gamma Walls → Large open interest at a specific strike acts as magnet or ceiling. If dealers are long calls at a strike above market, they sell into rallies approaching that level ("gamma wall" capping the move). Heavy put OI below = potential support or accelerant depending on dealer positioning.
Post-Expiration Fragility → Options pinning ends at monthly/quarterly expiry. The market can make a directional move immediately after the pin releases. If fundamentals deteriorated during the pinned period, the break can be sharp.

(crash insurance cheap or expensive) | Are dealers long or short gamma? (will moves be dampened or amplified) | What's the nearest gamma wall? (magnet / resistance level)

Path-Dependent Asset-Liability Framework

Credit crises don't just happen — they follow a path. The mismatch between asset duration and liability duration is the structural fault line.

Duration Mismatch Risk

Banks/institutions borrow short-term (deposits, commercial paper) and lend long-term (mortgages, corporate loans). When short rates rise:

  • → Funding costs rise immediately
  • → Asset yields locked in at low rates
  • → Net interest margin compresses
  • → Mark-to-market losses on held assets

Example: SVB 2023 — long UST / MBS funded with short-term deposits

Refinancing / Roll-Over Risk

When short-term debt must be rolled at much higher rates. The "maturity wall" — large amounts of debt maturing simultaneously into a high-rate environment creates systemic pressure.

  • → Monitor corporate debt maturities by year
  • → CRE (commercial real estate) a key risk
  • → Private credit opacity adds hidden risk
  • → EM sovereign rollover risk in USD debt

Traditional Credit Cycle vs. Modern (Path-Dependent) View

Traditional linear view

1. Expansion → credit growth, standards loosen
2. Peak → leverage maxes out, rates rise
3. Contraction → defaults rise, credit tightens
4. Trough → deleveraging, conditions reset

Modern path-dependent view

The crisis outcome depends on how you arrived at the stress, not just that stress exists. Key questions:

  • → How fast did rates rise? (velocity matters)
  • → What is the collateral chain? (cross-collateralization)
  • → Who holds the duration risk? (banks vs. market)
  • → What is the policy backstop capacity?
  • → Are there concentrated refinancing maturities?

Reflexivity & Forward-Looking Risk Framework

Markets are adaptive systems — not clockwork cycles. Expectations change outcomes. The path-dependent approach "wires redundancy triggers in advance so the next liquidity squeeze becomes an execution checklist, not a surprise."

Reflexivity in Action

→ Self-fulfilling crisis: If investors fear a funding squeeze, they pull credit early — making the squeeze happen even if fundamentals didn't demand it
→ Self-averting crisis: If investors deleverage preemptively on warnings, imbalances clear before they blow up
→ Policy reflexivity: CB credibility signals themselves change behavior — a credible inflation-fighter causes markets to tighten for them
→ Key implication: Don't dismiss warning signals because no blow-up followed. The best warnings are often never confirmed — the market reacted and averted the crisis.

Forward-Looking Checklist

→ Who refinances when? Map maturity walls by sector for next 12–18 months
→ At what rate? If rates are 300bp+ higher than original issuance, who can't afford it?
→ Who holds duration risk? Banks (systemic) vs. market (mark-to-market, not systemic)
→ Policy backstop capacity? How does Fed/FDIC intervention change the reflexive loop?
→ Have borrowers termed out? If yes, the "inevitable" crisis may be 2–3 years away, not imminent

Concrete example (from primer): A real estate firm borrows via 1-year commercial paper to fund long-term property. During low rates, it rolls easily each year. The moment the Fed hikes 500bp, the annual refinancing becomes prohibitively expensive — the firm sells properties at a discount, banks see collateral values fall, and pull credit from the whole sector. Traditional view saw an expansion; path-dependent view flagged the duration mismatch and maturity window as the structural fault line.

Related: See Global Liquidity → Credit & Liquidity Cycle Phases for the full phase-based framework (Expansion → Euphoria → Contraction → Recovery) and When Central Banks Lose Control for tail-scenario reflexivity (UK gilt 2022, Sept 2019 repo blowup).

Why the Traditional Credit Cycle Framework Fails

Poor Timing Precision

"Historical precedent doesn't help predict the exact date a boom ends." Warning signs (rapid credit growth, leverage) are necessary but not sufficient — they confirm a crisis is possible, never when. The framework describes phases after they occur.

Over-Simplification

Real crises can jump from calm to crisis (COVID March 2020) without following textbook phases. Assuming gradual progression is dangerous. Idiosyncratic shocks don't fit the mold.

Policy Intervention Short-Circuits the Cycle

CBs actively prevent "panic" phases via QE, rate cuts, bailouts. The expected cleansing bust may never fully materialize. 1998 (LTCM), 2008, 2020 all showed Fed truncating the cycle. The model assumes a cycle policy won't allow to complete.

One-Size-Fits-All

Each cycle differs: housing debt (2008), corporate leverage (2015 EM), EM carry trades (1997 Asia), pandemic (2020). Treating them as generic "expansion → bust" ignores structural differences that determine who is actually vulnerable.

Better question to ask: Instead of "Where are we in the cycle?" → "What stresses exist right now, and how do they unfold under different policy paths?"

Consumer Macroeconomic Playbook

Debt service ratio, permanent income hypothesis, and spending behavior by macro regime

What Drives Consumer Spending

Permanent Income Hypothesis (PIH)

Consumers smooth spending based on expected lifetime income, not just current income. Temporary windfalls (tax rebates, bonuses) are largely saved. Permanent income changes (job loss, wage growth) drive real spending shifts.

Life-Cycle Hypothesis (Modigliani): Sister theory — consumers smooth spending over their entire life span (borrow young → save in prime years → dissave in retirement). Together with PIH, this explains why stimulus checks get saved (one-off) but a raise gets spent (permanent).

Implication: One-off fiscal stimulus has diminishing multiplier; structural wage growth matters more.

Debt Service Ratio (DSR)

DSR = (Mortgage + consumer debt payments) / Disposable income. When DSR rises above ~15%, discretionary spending compresses. High DSR = household balance sheets stretched = consumer-led recession risk.

Watch: Fed's Household DSR series. Mortgage DSR vs. consumer credit DSR tell different stories.

Wealth Effect

Rising asset prices (housing, equities) boost consumer confidence and willingness to spend. Roughly: $1 of stock wealth = $0.03–0.05 in spending; $1 of housing wealth = $0.05–0.08 in spending (housing wealth effect is stronger).

Why housing > stocks: More households own homes; housing feels more "permanent" to consumers.

Consumption as the Transmission Mechanism

Consumer spending is ~60–70% of GDP. Research finds shocks originating in household consumption demand account for ~40% of US business cycle fluctuations since the 1970s. Consumption is both the engine and the conduit through which monetary/fiscal policy reaches the real economy.

Monetary Transmission to Consumer

1. Intertemporal substitution. Lower real rates → save less, spend more. Empirical effect is modest (elasticity is low); most consumers don't fine-tune spending vs small rate moves.
2. Cash-flow effect. Lower rates → smaller debt service for variable-rate borrowers (HELOC, ARM mortgages, credit cards). Frees disposable income immediately. Strong, fast, asymmetric — hits indebted households more.
3. Wealth effect. Lower rates → higher asset prices → richer households feel wealthier → spend more (especially top decile).
4. Big-ticket sensitivity. Auto loans, mortgages most rate-sensitive. Auto sales spike with 0% financing deals; housing turnover slumps when 30-yr fixed rises sharply.

Fiscal Transmission to Consumer

Direct payments / tax rebates. Highly variable multiplier. PIH predicts mostly saved unless perceived permanent. 2009 rebates largely paid down debt; 2020-21 COVID checks initially saved, then spent as restrictions eased.
Automatic stabilizers. Unemployment insurance, progressive taxation, food stamps support spending when income falls. Quiet but powerful — prevent deep cutbacks without explicit policy action.
Targeting matters. Money to liquidity-constrained households (low income, high MPC) spends more than money to high-savers. This is why the "excess savings" framework became central post-COVID.
The feedback loop: Stimulus → spending → demand pull → inflation → policy tightens → consumption cooled. The 2021-23 cycle is the textbook case.

The Self-Reinforcing Cycle

Weak spending → falling business revenue → layoffs → falling income → weaker spending. This downward spiral is what turns slowdowns into recessions. The 1990 and 2001 recessions were mild partly because consumer spending kept growing even as investment fell. 2008–09 was severe because consumption actually declined — a rarity that made the financial shock into a depression-adjacent event. Once consumers en masse decide to repair balance sheets, no amount of policy stimulus easily reverses it.

Historical Case Studies

2008–09: Great Recession Deleveraging

Housing bust destroyed household wealth, consumption actually fell (rare). Households deleveraged for years — propensity to consume out of extra income collapsed. Fed easing alone couldn't restart spending until balance sheets healed. Recovery was slow and uneven. Lesson: when consumers prioritize balance-sheet repair, policy stimulus has diminished effect.

2020–22: COVID Stimulus & Composition Shift

Saving rate spiked to ~30% during lockdowns + stimulus. "Excess savings" stockpile fueled goods boom 2020-21, then "revenge spending" on services 2022. By 2023, excess savings depleted for bottom half; top half still buffered. Lesson: direct fiscal transfers transmit fast; composition of spending shifts dramatically; effects asymmetric by income.

1970s: Great Inflation & Stagflation

Inflation hit double digits, mortgage rates >15% by 1981. "Buy now before prices rise" mentality fed back into more inflation. Volcker forced 1981-82 recession (10%+ unemployment) to break expectations. Lesson: once consumers + businesses lose faith in price stability, behavior perpetuates the cycle. Only painful policy resets expectations.

Building a Falsifiable Investment Thesis

7-step scientific framework and 9-point checklist for rigorous macro investing

First-Principles Thinking & The Conjecture-Refutation Cycle

Before any of the 7 steps below: develop the thesis itself by working from fundamentals, not narratives. Inspired by physicist David Deutsch's framework — "good explanations are deep, causal, and falsifiable" — treat every investment idea as a scientific hypothesis. The goal isn't to be right; it's to have a view that can be proven wrong.

First-Principles Method (Build the Thesis)

1. Strip narrative. Drop buzzwords ("disruption," "AI revolution," "this changes everything"). Get to the underlying economic mechanism.
2. Identify fundamental drivers. Equities: earnings, competitive moat, demand. Bonds: rates, inflation, credit. Commodities: supply/demand/inventories. Macro: liquidity, growth, policy.
3. Build a causal chain. "Demand exceeds supply → inventory drawdown → prices rise." Each link should be observable and testable, not assumed.
4. State the explanation, then attack it. "What would break this chain?" Find the weakest link before the market does.

Conjecture → Test → Refute → Learn (Run the Cycle)

Conjecture: Form the hypothesis with first-principles drivers and a testable prediction.
Test: Deploy capital (a real-world experiment) and observe data.
Refute or Confirm: If falsifying conditions trigger, exit immediately. If evidence corroborates, hold or scale.
Learn: Post-mortem every outcome. Was the thesis right (lucky vs skilled)? Wrong (assumption flaw vs unanticipated variable)? Each refuted thesis improves your model of how markets actually work.
Knowledge compounds like a portfolio. The losses you take in the process of refining theses are tuition.

"Good explanations" test (Deutsch): Is the thesis deep (addresses underlying mechanism, not surface description)? Causal (specifies why this drives that)? Falsifiable (states what would prove it wrong)? "Stock is going up because people are buying" fails on all three. "Lithium demand will outstrip supply as EV adoption accelerates, drawing inventories below X by Q3; if inventories build instead, thesis is dead" passes all three.

The 7-Step Falsifiable Thesis Framework

A great macro trade is a scientific hypothesis — it can be proven wrong. If you can't articulate what would disprove your thesis, it's not a thesis; it's a bias.

1

State the Core Claim

One clear sentence: "I believe X will happen because Y." The more specific the better. Vague theses ("the market is overvalued") can't be falsified.

2

Identify the Falsifying Conditions

What data or events would prove this wrong? Write them down before entering the trade. If you can't answer this, don't trade.

3

Assign Probabilities & Scenarios

What is the base case probability? What are the bear/bull alternative scenarios? How does the P&L look under each? Avoid binary thinking.

4

Define the Catalyst

What event or data release will cause the market to reprice? Without a catalyst, even a correct thesis can be a losing trade (timing is everything in macro).

5

Size for the Worst Case

Position size should reflect the scenario where you're wrong. Never size where a single loss wipes you out. Risk the amount you can explain and recover from.

6

Set a Time Horizon

When should the thesis play out? If the catalyst doesn't materialize by then, reassess — don't just hold and hope. Time horizon defines carry cost and patience budget.

7

Monitor and Update

Systematically track each data point that bears on your thesis. When falsifying conditions appear, exit. Don't fall in love with positions — fall in love with the process.

#QuestionWhy It Matters
1 What is the macro regime? (Growth ↑/↓, Inflation ↑/↓) Regime determines which assets have structural tailwinds. Don't fight the regime.
2 What is the global liquidity direction? (Fed + ECB + PBoC balance sheets) Liquidity is the tide that lifts/sinks all boats. Even correct theses fail in a liquidity squeeze.
3 What does the yield curve say? (Shape + direction of change) The curve is the market's consensus on future growth and policy. Respect it or have strong reason to fade it.
4 What is the USD doing? (Strong/weak, trend direction) USD direction drives EM capital flows, commodity prices, and global risk appetite. Strong USD = global tightening.
5 What is already priced in? (Futures, options, positioning) Markets move on surprise, not facts. Even a correct view loses if it's consensus. Check CFTC positioning.
6 What is the specific catalyst and timing? A thesis without a catalyst is a fantasy. NFP? FOMC? CPI? Earnings? Define the trigger.
7 What would prove me wrong? (The falsifier) Write this down. When it happens, act immediately. Most traders ignore falsifying conditions. Don't.
8 Is the risk/reward at least 2:1? (Max gain / max loss) Even at 50% hit rate, 2:1 R/R produces consistent long-run profits. Never take trades below 1.5:1.
9 What is the carry cost? (Positive or negative while waiting) Negative carry trades need to pay off fast or they bleed P&L. Know your patience budget before entry.

Multi-Timeframe Integration & The Cone of Uncertainty

A robust thesis usually has nested timeframes — short-term tactical sub-theses inside medium-term setups inside long-term structural views. Each timeframe gets its own falsification criteria and position sizing reflects the widening cone of uncertainty further out.

Short-term (days to weeks)

Tactical entries/exits around catalysts (FOMC, NFP, CPI, earnings). High signal-to-noise. Confidence: relatively high (event windows are narrow). Sizing: can be larger; risk is tightly bounded.

Falsifier examples: CPI prints opposite direction; FOMC dot plot moves against the trade; gap-down post-earnings.

Medium-term (months to 1-2 years)

Cycle-based theses: regime transitions, Fed cycle, earnings revisions trend, credit cycle phase. Most macro trades live here. Sizing: moderate; allow room for noise within the trend.

Falsifier examples: 2 consecutive quarters of EPS miss; regime quadrant shifts (e.g., overheat → stagflation); credit spreads break out of range.

Long-term (3-10 years)

Structural shifts: demographics, energy transition, monetary regime change. Lowest confidence, widest cone of uncertainty. Sizing: smaller, or expressed via options/structures with limited downside.

Falsifier examples: structural driver fails to materialize by N-year checkpoint; new technology obsoletes the thesis; policy regime change.

Multi-Timeframe Discipline

Set checkpoints at each horizon (e.g., "By month 6: see initial revenue acceleration; by month 18: margin expansion; by year 5: 2x earnings"). Evaluate the evidence at each. Strong opinions, weakly held — hold conviction in the thesis but the conviction itself should be tentative and ready to update. When short-term and long-term signals diverge, investigate which is the noise and which is the new information. The cone of uncertainty widens with time; honor that with smaller size on long-horizon bets.

Three pairs across rates, equities, and commodities. The left column is what most traders carry around in their heads; the right is what discipline looks like.

❌ Rates — Narrative

"The government printed trillions of dollars; this must cause hyperinflation and bond yields will skyrocket. Short Treasuries." Why it fails: no timing, no specific trigger, assumes the transmission rather than testing it. This was the Japanese "widowmaker" trade — investors shorting JGBs for decades on high debt + easy policy. Japan had deflationary forces; yields stayed low. Losses repeated.

✅ Rates — Falsifiable

"Core CPI >4% YoY with sticky services inflation. Hypothesis: Fed forced to hold or hike, pushing 2Y above 5% in 6 months. Falsifier: if CPI prints <0.2% MoM for 3 consecutive readings OR Fed signals cuts at any FOMC, thesis is dead and I cover the short." Testable, time-bound, with explicit exit triggers.

❌ Equities — Narrative

"This company will be the next Amazon — it's revolutionizing its industry. I'm in for the long haul." Why it fails: Analogy substituting for analysis, no metrics, no timeframe, no exit signal short of bankruptcy. Every missed quarter gets rationalized as "growing pains." Result: ride to zero.

✅ Equities — Falsifiable

"Growing revenue at 30% with improving unit economics. Hypothesis: 25-30% annual revenue growth for 2 years, operating margins turn positive in Q4, driven by network effects in niche categories → fair value 50% higher. Falsifier: growth decelerates <15% for 2 quarters, OR CAC rises so margins can't improve → exit." Clear drivers, measurable checkpoints, defined exit.

❌ Commodities — Narrative

"Oil is going up — supply is tight, China is reopening, my gut says $120." Why it fails: Gut feel masquerading as analysis. No specific supply/demand mechanics, no inventory data, no exit. When prices stall or pull back, no framework for what to do.

✅ Commodities — Falsifiable

"Global demand recovering to pre-pandemic; supply constrained by underinvestment + OPEC discipline. Hypothesis: inventory drawdowns >X mb/month for 12 months → oil into $90-100. Falsifier: 2-3 weeks of inventory builds OR major economy enters recession → exit, expect <$70." Weekly EIA inventory data is the test; recession indicators are the kill switch.

When This Framework Applies — Risk-Premium vs Knowledge-Based Investing

Not every dollar needs a thesis. There are two fundamentally different ways to make money in markets — and the entire methodology in this tab is for the second one.

Risk-Premium Investing (Buy & Hold)

Earn long-run expected returns by simply holding diversified beta — an S&P 500 index fund, 60/40, a global multi-asset portfolio. Returns are compensation for bearing risk; no thesis required. Inflexible (struggles in regime shifts), but easy and low-cost.

Right approach when: you don't have edge, can't actively monitor positions, or want low decision overhead.

Knowledge-Based Thesis Investing

Earn returns in excess of risk premia by applying knowledge, theses, and tactical adjustment. Requires the discipline in this tab — first principles, falsification, monitoring, iteration. Higher effort, potentially higher risk-adjusted returns, dynamic to regime changes.

Right approach when: you have edge (specialized knowledge, time to monitor), want to seek alpha, or want to manage risk tightly through regime shifts.

Honest self-assessment: if you don't have time to research, set falsifiers, log evidence, and execute exits, you're better off taking the risk premium and accepting drawdowns. The worst outcome is doing active investing without the discipline — paying alpha costs while earning beta returns minus mistakes.

Common Thesis Failures & How to Avoid Them

Narrative Without Data

A compelling story that isn't anchored in measurable indicators. Fix: require at least 3 specific data series that must confirm the view before sizing up.

Ignoring the Regime

Correct fundamental view, wrong macro backdrop. E.g., being long growth stocks during Stagflation because "earnings are good." The regime overrides individual fundamentals.

No Exit Plan

Entering with a thesis but no defined exit — neither profit target nor stop. Result: holding through full reversal while convincing yourself the thesis is "still valid."

Confusing Correlation With Causation

Two things moved together last cycle. They may not next cycle if the transmission mechanism has changed (e.g., stock-bond correlation regime shift in high inflation).

Anchoring to Entry Price

Defending a position because you're underwater. The market doesn't care where you entered. Re-evaluate: would you put this trade on today at current levels? If no, exit.

Sizing for the Best Case

Position sized as if the bull case is guaranteed. When the base case or bear case materializes, losses exceed the budget. Always size for the downside scenario.
